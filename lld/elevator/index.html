Elevator system

Several elevators, hall buttons on every floor, and a dispatcher that decides which car answers each call. The interesting parts are the movement rule (keep going in one direction while there are stops ahead) and the dispatch cost function.

Low-level designConcurrency

Picture it. A team of couriers on one long street. A courier already heading north picks up a parcel further north on the way; a parcel behind them goes to whichever courier can reach it soonest, not necessarily the nearest one.

Requirements

Functional

  • Hall calls: a floor and a direction (up or down) from the buttons on each floor.
  • Car calls: a floor pressed inside a particular elevator.
  • Assign each hall call to the elevator that can serve it soonest.
  • Each elevator serves stops in its current direction before reversing (the LOOK algorithm).

Non-functional

  • Calls arrive from many threads (button panels) while the controller is moving cars.
  • Every call is eventually served; no elevator is assigned work it can't reach.
  • The dispatch policy can be swapped (Strategy).

Entities and relationships

  • Movement (LOOK): each tick, if the car is at a stop it opens its doors and removes the stop. Otherwise it keeps its direction while there are stops ahead, reverses when there aren't, and goes idle when it has none.
  • Dispatch cost (NearestCarCost):
    • idle car: the distance to the call;
    • moving toward the call in the same direction: the distance;
    • anything else: distance to its turning point (furthest stop), then back to the call. Ties go to the lower id.
  • States are implicit in direction: IDLE, MOVING_UP, MOVING_DOWN, plus a doors-open tick at each stop.

Design patterns used

  • Strategy: DispatchStrategy makes the assignment rule pluggable (nearest car, zoning, least busy).
  • State: an elevator's behaviour depends on its direction; a full version adds door states and maintenance mode.
  • Command: hall and car calls are request objects queued for the controller.

How a request flows

A real controller runs one loop per elevator and reacts to sensors and timers; the logic is the same, but the tick is driven by hardware events rather than a for loop.

Concurrency

  • hallCall, carCall and tick are synchronized on the system: assignment reads every elevator's state, so it must not interleave with a step.
  • Button panels are producers; the controller is the single consumer that changes elevator state, a simple and safe split.
  • For many elevators, each could own its lock and run its own thread; then dispatch reads a snapshot of each car, and a car re-checks its assignment when it picks it up.
  • Requests counted as served when the assigned car stops at that floor; the demo checks none are lost under concurrent calls.

Cost of each operation

OperationTimeWhy
hallCallO(E)One cost evaluation per elevator.
tickO(E log S)Each elevator looks up its next stop in a sorted set.
carCallO(log S)Insert into the elevator's stop set.

Code

Common mistakes

  • Sending the nearest elevator regardless of its direction, so it turns around mid-journey.
  • Serving stops in arrival order (FIFO), which zig-zags the car up and down.
  • Letting button threads modify elevator state directly while the controller is stepping it.
  • Forgetting the idle state, so a car with no stops keeps moving.

Interview follow-ups

  • Capacity and weight limits: skip a hall call when full.
  • Express elevators and zoning (floors 0–20 vs 21–40).
  • Peak modes: park idle cars at the lobby in the morning.
  • Emergency and maintenance states, and how they override dispatch.
